`Yield to maturity (YTM)` is often quoted for bonds - government as well as corporate bonds - as a metric that summarizes the return on an investment on these securities when held for its life, in other words, until maturity. Atleast the just initiated buyers of bonds may interpret it that way. This is far from the truth.

YTM is the rate of interest actualized on two meeting of two conditions: 1. the bond is held until maturity (for its life) and 2. The coupons received are reinvested at the same interest rate either in the same instrument or elsewhere. These two conditions underpin the valuation of any given bond. Thus, the YTM is the internal rate of return (IRR) of the bond's cash flows. It is the discount rate that makes the present value of all future cash flows equal to the current price of the bond.

The latter assumption is rarely met in real life. It is possible that the coupon is invested in a relatively higher yielding instrument and thus notching up the final return and it is also possible that the alternatives are no good.

The likelihood of failure on either of the conditions should inform the investor that the YTM on record is too good to be true and that it is upper bound on the rate of interest.
