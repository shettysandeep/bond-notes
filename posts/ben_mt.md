The RBI hiked the repo rate by 25 bps in early October, increased the CRR to maintenance limit to 99%
and imposed other restrictions to counter the inflationary pressures in the
economy.

A tighter liquidity raises the cost of money and depresses real output and
prices over time. At a sectoral level, the effects play out in a staggered way.

The literature on monetary transmission provide insight on what to expect.
Bernanke and Gertler (1995) summarise: consumer durables spending and
residential investments, the most interest-sensitive part of aggregate demand,
will fall quickly and possibly sharply, followed closely by consumer
non-durables.

Production declines follow with a lag, first inventory rises in the short run
and then it begins to fall as companies pull back on investments and production.
The inventory disinvestment eventually accounts for a large portion of the
decline in GDP.

One thing to note Bernanke and Gertler (2003) look at unanticipated shocks. A
sudden change in policy stance. Here, the markets were expecting a rate hike by
the RBI as early as July based on the bond markets, where yields on the
benchmark rose over 30-60 bps. In this case the price and output adjustment may
chart a different path than described above. That said, for the real
sectors of the economy this move may still be an unanticipated one.

The underlying inflationary pressures remain unchanged and seem to be further
compounded by poor monsoons likely dampening rural income and spending in the
upcoming quarters. We will have to wait and watch what more actions the central
bank takes and how output and prices adjust to the policy tightening.
