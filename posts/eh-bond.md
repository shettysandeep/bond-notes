## what is a bond?

_Each bond_ promises a certain amount of money paid periodically for a certain number of periods. The periodic amount paid is called the `coupon`, the number of periods it is paid for is called the `term` or `maturity`, and a large amount paid at the end of the term is called the `face value` or `principal`. The frequency of the coupon payments can be annual, semi-annual, quarterly, or monthly, and the `face value`, 'principal', or 'redemption value' can also be paid in chunks over time instead of a lumpsum at the end.

## pricing of a bond

To buy such a stream of cashflow as laid out in a bond, a buyer has to decide how much to pay for it - that is the investment amount or the buy price of the bond. The general practice to arrive is to discount the cash flows - the coupon and face value - at a discount rate or an interest rate of choice. This will arrive at what is called as the present value of the cashflow encapsulated in a bond. This `present value` or today's value of the cashflows tells you what you must start with today and if you receieve the selected discount rate or the interest rate of choice you will have the total cashflow the bond under study promises to pay during its life and at maturity.

That discount rate you have selected is the yield to maturity that you want for your investment. However, the seller of the bond or the issuer would require a different level of investment from you and will thus offer a different YTM for your investment. So, if you find the offered YTM attractive you can invest if not then look elsewhere for opportunities.

## discount rate - ytm

The key point to take away from here is that the discount rate you used to calculate your investment or purchase price for this bond is making an important assumption. That is of reinvestment rate. This basically says that the coupons or any cash flow you receive during the life of the bond you are reinvesting that amount at the exact discount rate or interest rate of your choice. This is basically the compounding magic. The money compounds because the coupons themselves earn interest - also known as 'interest on interest'. The catch here is exactly that. Your calculated or desired YTM assumes that the coupon or cashflow received gets reinvested at the same rate. However, in reality this may not be the case in most instances. The interests will earn lower because of multiple constraints - say alternative investments don't offer such an attractive rate or if available you need sufficient size to arrive before you can invest or maybe you take on more risks for that higher returns. In effect the actual return on the bond investment is likely to be lower than the YTM listed on the bond.
